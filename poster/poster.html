1Introduction

Everything runs on the grid

Hospitals, water and desalination, cooling, metro and airports, banks, telecom and data centres all stop without power, and so do factories and cold storage. A grid failure hits a whole economy at once, so it is critical national infrastructure.

External attackers have already shown it can be done. In 2015, hackers opened breakers at 30 substations in Ukraine and cut power to about 225,000 customers.

EVs are going mainstream, and so are chargers

  • Over 20% of new cars sold worldwide in 2024 were electric; the IEA expects over 40% by 2030.
  • UAE policy targets EVs at about 10% of the fleet by 2030 and 50% by 2050.
  • Dubai had 47,944 EVs and about 1,860 public charge points at the end of 2025.
  • ADNOC and TAQA aim for 70,000 charge points in Abu Dhabi by 2030.

2Problem Statement

Thousands of chargers, one weak point.

  1. Control the chargers, control the loadA charger draws 7 kW at home and up to 150 kW at a fast-charging station. An attacker who takes over thousands, one by one or through the operator's cloud backend, never needs to touch a power station.
  2. Easy to compromise, little oversightCheap networked devices in car parks and driveways. Many makers, protocols and software versions: security varies by company, with no single baseline every charger must meet.
  3. Together they can destabilise the gridHacked chargers raise their rate at once, just under the level where each car's battery management would cut the charge. Demand jumps faster than power plants can respond, feeders trip and frequency falls until customers are cut off. Worst overnight, when cars are plugged in.
  4. The cost is nationalLost production, spoiled food and medicine, stalled ports, data centres and payments, then restoration. The 2003 North America blackout (not a cyberattack) hit about 50 million people and cost an estimated $6 to 10 billion.

3Our Solution

We assume some chargers will be hacked, and protect the grid: detect the artificial surge and throttle it before breakers or frequency relays trip.

1
Watch the feeder, not the chargerTracks how fast load rises and whether it keeps drifting above plan. Works even if chargers and backend are both compromised.
2
Check requests against what was authorisedFar more than scheduled, and many chargers at once, signals attack. A normal tariff surge was scheduled, so it passes.
3
Coordinate nationallyIf several feeders are hit together, the alert spreads to the rest.

Staged response

Watchraise alert
Throttleforce back to schedule
Isolateslow 6 A charge
Releasegradual return
Enforcement runs on an independent hardware gateway at each charger, so a hacked charger cannot ignore it.

  • Daytime attacks are far weaker: cars are not plugged in.

7Impact & next steps

Why it matters

  • Fits the UAE National Cybersecurity Strategy: critical-infrastructure protection, detection and response, secure IoT.
  • Scales as chargers multiply, because it watches the feeder, not each device.

Next steps

  • ESP32 hardware gateway: current sensing, independent pilot-signal limiter, contactor, signed commands.
  • Validate on real utility data and a hardware-in-the-loop bench.
  • Integrate OCPP, IEC 61850, DNP3 and PMU data.
