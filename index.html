Introduction

Everything runs on the grid

Almost everything a country does depends on its power grid. Hospitals, water and desalination, air conditioning in a desert climate, metro and traffic systems, airports, ports, banks, telecom networks and data centres all stop within minutes or hours without it. So do factories, refineries and cold-storage warehouses, where an outage means idle production lines and spoiled stock, not just inconvenience.

The grid is also shared by everyone. A failure doesn't hit one customer, it hits a whole economy at once, which is why it is treated as critical national infrastructure. That makes it a natural target for anyone who wants to do harm from the outside, whether criminal groups, hostile states or activists, and it has already happened. In December 2015, attackers remotely opened breakers at 30 substations in Ukraine and cut power to about 225,000 customers for several hours. Keeping external actors away from the grid is a national security job as much as an engineering one.

EVs are going mainstream, and so are chargers

Electric cars are no longer a niche. Worldwide they made up more than 20% of new car sales in 2024, and the IEA expects over 40% by 2030 on current policies. The UAE's national EV policy aims for EVs to be about 10% of the fleet by 2030 and 50% by 2050, and Dubai alone ended 2025 with 47,944 registered EVs.

Every EV needs somewhere to plug in, so chargers are multiplying with them: home wall units, public AC posts and DC fast chargers. Dubai had roughly 1,860 public charging points by the end of 2025, and ADNOC and TAQA are aiming for 70,000 charge points in Abu Dhabi by 2030. Each one is a high-power, internet-connected device wired straight into the grid.

Problem statement

Thousands of chargers, one weak point

Control of chargers is control of load

A charger draws anywhere from about 7 kW at home to 150 kW at a fast-charging station, as much as several houses. An attacker who takes over thousands of them, one by one or through the cloud backend that manages them, doesn't need to touch a power station or a substation. The chargers are the weapon.

Easy to compromise, little oversight

Chargers are cheap networked computers placed in car parks, apartment blocks and driveways where nobody inspects them. They come from many manufacturers and operators, running different protocols and software versions. Security requirements differ from company to company and market to market, and there is rarely one baseline every charger must meet before it connects to the grid. Reviews of EV charger security keep finding weaknesses in firmware, web interfaces, billing and remote-management links.

Together they can destabilise the grid

A grid has to balance supply and demand every second. If compromised chargers all raise their rate at the same moment, demand jumps faster than power plants can respond. Local feeders overload and trip, and if the jump is large enough, frequency falls until automatic load shedding cuts customers off. The attacker stays just under the rate at which each car's battery management would cut the charge, so the cars keep drawing power, and it works best overnight when most cars are plugged in.

In our model of a 2040 UAE fleet, roughly 600,000 compromised chargers (about 40% of those installed) are enough to start load shedding nationwide, and about a quarter of the chargers on one EV-dense Dubai feeder can trip its breaker.

The economic cost

A blackout costs far more than the electricity not sold: lost production, spoiled food and medicine, stalled ports and transport, interrupted data centres and payments, and the work of restoring the network. For scale, the 2003 Northeast blackout in North America, which was not a cyberattack, left about 50 million people without power and was estimated to cost roughly $6 to 10 billion depending on the study. An attacker also chooses the timing, and can aim at the hours when the grid has the least slack.

There is lasting damage too: tourism and investor confidence, and trust in EVs themselves just as the country is trying to move people onto them. We have not modelled UAE-specific costs; these figures are reference points.

Our approach. Instead of trying to make every charger unhackable, SENTINEL protects the grid. It watches each feeder for an artificial surge in charging and throttles it before breakers or frequency relays trip. How SENTINEL decides

Watches the feeder, not the charger

It tracks how fast charging load is rising on each feeder and whether it keeps drifting above plan. This works even if the chargers and the charging backend are both compromised.

Checks requests against what was authorised

A charger asking for far more power than the backend scheduled, and many doing it within seconds of each other, is a strong sign of attack. A normal tariff-start surge was scheduled, so it is left alone.

Throttles in stages

Watch, then throttle (force chargers back to their schedule), then isolate (quarantine offenders at a slow 6 A charge), then release gradually. A national coordinator spreads the alert if several feeders are hit at once.
